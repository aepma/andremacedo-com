§ 1 · the exchequer, westminster, about 1100 to 1826

For seven centuries England kept what it was owed in hazel. The proof was never the wood. It was the fit.

A clerk cut the sum across a stick, inked the payer’s name on it, and split it down its length through every cut. The payer walked out with the longer half, the stock; the Exchequer kept the shorter, the foil. Pare a notch away or add one, and your half no longer meets the other. A forgery needed both halves, and nobody held both.

§ 2 · the chequer · counted before it was cut

Nothing was cut until it had been counted, with counters on a dark cloth ruled like a chessboard. The Exchequer is named for the cloth.

The Dialogue of the Exchequer, written about 1179, sets the columns out from the right: pence, shillings, pounds, scores of pounds, hundreds, thousands. Twelve pence carried to a shilling, twenty shillings to a pound. When the counting was done the counters were swept off and the sum was cut, each column into its own shape of notch. The cloth kept nothing; the wood kept everything.

§ 3 · the pipe roll · what the foil’s side wrote down, 1130 to 1832

The wood proved each payment. The roll kept the account, and when nothing was left owing it closed the line with three words: et quietus est.

Twice a year every sheriff rendered his account at the Exchequer. Clerks wrote what he owed on sheets of parchment sewn head to foot and rolled. He laid his stocks against the Exchequer’s foils, and each one that fitted was allowed against the debt. If anything was left, the line ended et debet, and he owes. If nothing was, it ended et quietus est: and he is quit. The sticks were burned. The rolls were kept.

Quietus was the Exchequer’s word for a debt discharged before Hamlet used it for a life.

§ 4 · westminster, 16 october 1834

Eight years after the last tally was cut, the old ones were fed to the stoves under the House of Lords, and the stoves set fire to Parliament.

Two cartloads of what the country had once owed. The flues overheated, the panelling caught, and by night the Palace of Westminster was burning from end to end. Turner watched from the river and painted it. A record that has finished its work is still dry wood.
